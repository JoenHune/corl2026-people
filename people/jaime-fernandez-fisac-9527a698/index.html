Training Controllable World Models via Action Influence Maximization

Poster · 共同署名

该篇个人署名单位：Princeton University

摘要原文

Action-conditioned video generative models promise to serve as general-purpose world models for robotics, leveraging internet-scale data to simulate complex physical interactions. While these models excel at visual realism, they often struggle with controllability--reliably simulating diverse future outcomes from the same state under different action sequences. In this work, we identify a structural cause for this failure: world models trained on successful, goal-directed trajectories tend to ignore actions entirely, collapsing into success-biased video players that hallucinate desirable outcomes regardless of the input action. We characterize this lack of "persistence of excitation" as a collapse in empowerment. To remedy this, we propose an Action Influence Maximization (AIM) objective and Batch Online Reinforcement Learning algorithm that encourages controllability by biasing the training distribution toward state-action pairs that maximize mutual information gain. We evaluate our method extensively for training world models on robotic interaction data on simulated manipulation and navigation tasks. Across our experiments, we find that AIM significantly improves the controllability (and hence, fidelity) of world models in comparison to naively training on expert data. Most notably, world models trained with AIM can outperform the controllability of state-of-the-art world models trained with 85x more data for challenging robotic manipulation tasks.

Human-like autonomy emerges from self-play and a pinch of human data

Poster · 共同署名

该篇个人署名单位：Princeton University

摘要原文

Self-play reinforcement learning has recently taken the wheel in autonomous driving. The approach uses cheap, large-scale simulation to substitute expensive, large-scale human driving data used by imitation learning approaches. While cost-effective, policies trained through self-play often behave unpredictably around humans; they fail to coordinate because they tend to converge to effective but incompatible driving conventions. Previous works attempt to mitigate such behavioral incompatibilities through reward engineering and domain randomization techniques, which are brittle and labor-intensive. We build on the best of both approaches and instead train driving policies using 30 minutes of human driving data and over 60 years of self-play simulation with a minimal reward function that solely encodes safe goal reaching. We call this _spiced self-play_: just as a pinch of a well-chosen ingredient can transform a dish, a small amount of human data, added as a behavioral anchor, disproportionately improves coordination with human drivers. Results show that our policies achieve improved coordination with real human driving trajectories using 2,500× less human data than imitation learning baselines. The entire pipeline completes in 15 hours on a single consumer-grade GPU. Together, our results point toward a positive revision of the field's understanding of data scaling: a small quantity of human data may suffice to regularize the vast space of driving policies toward ones that fit the road.
