Human-like autonomy emerges from self-play and a pinch of human data

Poster · 首位署名

摘要原文

Self-play reinforcement learning has recently taken the wheel in autonomous driving. The approach uses cheap, large-scale simulation to substitute expensive, large-scale human driving data used by imitation learning approaches. While cost-effective, policies trained through self-play often behave unpredictably around humans; they fail to coordinate because they tend to converge to effective but incompatible driving conventions. Previous works attempt to mitigate such behavioral incompatibilities through reward engineering and domain randomization techniques, which are brittle and labor-intensive. We build on the best of both approaches and instead train driving policies using 30 minutes of human driving data and over 60 years of self-play simulation with a minimal reward function that solely encodes safe goal reaching. We call this _spiced self-play_: just as a pinch of a well-chosen ingredient can transform a dish, a small amount of human data, added as a behavioral anchor, disproportionately improves coordination with human drivers. Results show that our policies achieve improved coordination with real human driving trajectories using 2,500× less human data than imitation learning baselines. The entire pipeline completes in 15 hours on a single consumer-grade GPU. Together, our results point toward a positive revision of the field's understanding of data scaling: a small quantity of human data may suffice to regularize the vast space of driving policies toward ones that fit the road.

Self-play is not enough: human imitation improves self-play autonomy in the wild

Poster · 共同署名

摘要原文

Self-play reinforcement learning at scale has emerged as a powerful paradigm for training autonomous driving policies, producing agents that exhibit robust collision-avoidance and stable navigation in simulation. However, policies trained purely through self-play lack exposure to the distribution of real human driving behavior, resulting in trajectories that appear unnatural. To overcome this, we present Self-play with Human Imitation Fine-Tuning (SHIFT), a hybrid training framework that fine-tunes a pretrained self-play foundation policy on real-world driving demonstrations without changing the underlying reinforcement learning algorithm. SHIFT fine-tunes in closed-loop log-replay simulation, in which the ego vehicle is rolled out by the learned policy while the non-ego agents are replayed from logged human trajectories. Distance to the human expert trajectory is simply an additional reward term in the same PPO with advantage filtering algorithm used for pretraining. To enable extended fine-tuning without losing the base self-play performance, we use a KL regularization loss against the frozen self-play foundation policy. On the closed-loop nuPlan Val14 benchmark, SHIFT improves over the self-play baseline in human-likeness (ADE: 9.4 m → 8.0 m, FDE: 21.3 m → 18.5 m), safety (collision rate: 6.4% → 3.9%, drivable area violations: 2.5% → 1.7%, red light violations: 1.6% → 1.0%), and comfort (brake tap rate: 29% → 20%, oscillation rate: 9% → 4%), and achieves the best ADE, FDE, collision, and drivable area scores among all baselines, including extended self-play, behavior cloning, BC+RL, and human-regularized PPO. We deploy both policies on self-driving cars and find similar improvements in the real world (2.6× fewer safety takeovers: one every 0.57 → 1.46 miles). To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of self-play and self-play with human imitation on public roads. Our results suggest that post-training self-play with human imitation, stabilized by KL regularization, is a promising direction for deployable autonomous driving systems. An open-source implementation, along with additional materials, will be available on the project page: https://rohit-ash.github.io/SHIFT/

Scaling Self-Play for End-to-End Driving

Poster · 共同署名

摘要原文

End-to-end autonomous driving models are typically trained on offline human-demonstration datasets that provide limited state coverage and often no closed-loop feedback, making them prone to compounding errors when deployed in closed-loop and brittle to long-tail agent interactions. To overcome these limitations, we propose an alternative strategy for training end-to-end driving models: large-scale self-play directly from pixels in simulation. While prior self-play approaches have shown promising transfer to real-world driving, they typically assume vectorized Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) observations that are incompatible with end-to-end policies operating directly on sensor observations. To this end, we introduce \emph{Gigapixel}, a high-throughput batched driving simulator with perspective rendering, enabling scalable self-play directly from pixel observations. Rather than targeting compute-costly photorealistic sensor simulation, Gigapixel renders a simplified bounding-box world that preserves essential scene structure while achieving throughput at 50k agent steps per second. Since direct pixel-space self-play RL is prohibitively sample-inefficient at end-to-end model scale, we propose \emph{self-play DAgger} training: we train pixel-based policies in self-play via on-policy distillation from a privileged RL teacher. To bridge the sim-to-real gap, we subsequently transfer the self-play trained policies to real-world sensor data through lightweight perception adaptation. Policies trained in Gigapixel and adapted to real-world sensor data achieve competitive performance on the HUGSIM and NAVSIM-v2 benchmarks without human trajectory supervision. Moreover, scaling self-play training yields proportional gains in policy performance, establishing self-play as a practical and scalable strategy for training end-to-end models.
